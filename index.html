Plus, the Arab Spring was already reshaping the Middle East, and ignoring Libya could mean losing influence in the region.

The turning point came in March 2011 when the UN Security Council authorized military action to protect civilians. NATO launched Operation Odyssey Dawn, with the U.S. leading the initial airstrikes. The goal was to degrade Gaddafi’s military capabilities and create a buffer zone for rebel forces.

However, the U.S. quickly handed over command to NATO, hoping to distance itself from direct combat roles. Looking back, the decision to intervene in Libya appears to have been fraught with unintended consequences. The initial hope of a quick, clean operation turned into a messy, ongoing conflict. Critics argue that the lack of a coherent post-conflict plan left Libya vulnerable to chaos and extremism.

Fable 5.1

The Libya Thing: How Obama Went to War Without Really Meaning To

Quick refresher on where the world was in February 2011. Tunisia’s dictator had just fled. Mubarak had resigned on the 11th after eighteen days of Tahrir Square. The whole region felt like it was tipping, and the White House had spent weeks trying to look like it was on the right side of that.

Then Libya happened.

Protests started in Benghazi around February 15. Gaddafi didn’t do the Mubarak thing and step aside. He went on TV, called the protesters rats and cockroaches, and promised to hunt them down house by house. The east of the country fell to rebels almost immediately, and for a couple of weeks it looked like he was finished.

He wasn’t. By early March, his forces had regrouped and were rolling the rebels back town by town along the coast road. By mid-March, they were closing on Benghazi, a city of roughly 700,000 people, and Gaddafi was saying out loud that there’d be no mercy.

Why get involved at all

This is the part worth sitting with, because the administration was split badly. Gates, Biden, and the Joint Chiefs were against it. Their argument was basically: this isn’t a vital U.S. interest, we’re already in two wars, and no one has a plan for what comes after. Clinton, Susan Rice, and Samantha Power were for it, and their argument was Rwanda and Srebrenica. Power had literally written the book on America standing by during genocides. Rice had been at the NSC in 1994. For that camp, letting Benghazi happen would be the defining moral failure of the presidency.

Then the incentives stacked up in a way that made “no” hard to say. France and Britain were pushing hard (Sarkozy especially). On March 12, the Arab League itself asked for a no-fly zone, which gave political cover nobody expected. And the whole thing looked cheap: air power only, no ground troops, allies doing most of the flying. Obama could act, avoid another Iraq, and not be the guy who cheered Tahrir Square and then watched a massacre.

He made the call the evening of March 15.

The war

March 17: UN Security Council Resolution 1973 passes, authorizing “all necessary measures” to protect civilians. Russia, China, Germany, Brazil, and India abstained rather than veto, which matters later.

March 19: French jets hit Gaddafi’s armor outside Benghazi. That night the U.S. and UK fired over a hundred Tomahawks at Libyan air defenses. The U.S. ran the opening phase, then handed command to NATO on March 31. An anonymous adviser described the approach to The New Yorker as “leading from behind,” and that phrase stuck to Obama for the rest of his presidency.

It was supposed to be days. It was seven months. The mission quietly became regime change; “protecting civilians” meant bombing anything Gaddafi’s side moved. In May the administration blew past the War Powers 60-day limit and argued this didn’t count as “hostilities,” which annoyed basically everyone in Congress.

Rebels took Tripoli in late August. On October 20, Gaddafi was pulled out of a drainage pipe in Sirte and killed. NATO wrapped up October 31. Zero American deaths, about $1 billion spent. On paper, a win.

After

There was no after. No stabilization force, no plan. The militias that toppled Gaddafi never disarmed. A year later, four Americans died in the attack on the Benghazi compound. By 2014, Libya was in a second civil war with two governments, ISIS held Sirte, and the country became the main launch point for migrant boats into Europe.

Asked in 2016 by Chris Wallace what his worst mistake was, Obama said: “Probably failing to plan for the day after what I think was the right thing to do in intervening in Libya.”

Fable 5.1, Sep 2026

The Ledger Is Not Your Friend

Crypto was pitched as a revolt against the banks. What we got was the banks’ logic with the humans removed.

Think about what money used to be: a medium for exchange between people. A way to get the grain to the miller and the bread to the table. Crypto takes that already-abstracted token and abstracts it again, until the only thing left is the number going up. There is no bread. There is no miller. There is a screen, and a dopamine loop, and someone who bought in before you.

This is the endgame of a mindset — the same one that gave us the stock ticker, the derivative, the algorithm that trades faster than thought. The point was never to build a better commons. The point was to escape the commons entirely: to find a place where the code is law and no messy human institution can ask you to share.

And the code isn’t neutral. It was written by people who believed trust itself was a bug. Every blockchain is a monument to the assumption that we cannot look each other in the eye. So we burn a nation’s worth of electricity to avoid a handshake.

The “stupid” part is simpler. A currency that nobody spends is not a currency. It’s a collectible. A collectible whose value depends entirely on recruiting the next believer is a chain letter with better graphics.

We were told this would free us. It has mostly freed the early adopters, and sold the rest of us a lottery ticket dressed as a philosophy.

Log off. Find a person. Trade something real.

For the other side: defenders argue crypto gives people in unstable currencies or under authoritarian regimes a way to hold and move value, enables cheaper cross-border remittances, and that its energy footprint has dropped sharply as major chains moved to proof-of-stake. They’d also say early speculative excess doesn’t disqualify a technology — the same was said of the internet in 2000.

So, as AI gets better at synthesizing the average of the internet, and as AI becomes smarter, humanness degrades.
