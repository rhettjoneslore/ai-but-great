Prompt: Tell the story of how the Brooklyn Bridge actually got built, start to finish, in about 700 words - the people, what it cost, what went wrong.

Clemens

This past weekend I visited the Brooklyn Bridge for the first time since high school. I crossed it walking, and I thought to myself that it was just as amazing now as it was when I walked across it twenty years ago. I also realized that I didn’t know anything about its history. So I read up a little bit. Turns out the story is a fascinating one — filled with visionary engineers, death, illness, corruption, tragedy, triumph, and the rise of the city we now call New York.

Back in the mid-1800s, NYC’s two largest cities (Manhattan and Brooklyn) weren’t connected to each other by any bridges. There was just a ferry connecting them. As NYC industrialized, it began to become clear that the ferry system wasn’t going to keep up with population growth. In 1867, the German immigrant John A. Roebling proposed a giant suspension bridge connecting the two cities. A suspension bridge was very novel at the time — in fact, this would have been the first big one ever built. But Roebling was confident that it could be done; he had already built a similar bridge in Cincinnati. In the 1870s, his designs for a much bigger bridge over New York Bay got the go-ahead.

In order to build the bridge, Roebling needed to build two big stone towers — one in Manhattan and one in Brooklyn. In order to do that, he had to dig deep into the riverbed to make sure the towers would hold steady against the tides. To dig into the bedrock under the East River, he invented huge caissons — essentially big boxes you lower down into the water until their tops break the surface. Workers would descend into the caissons and dig, using compressed air to keep the water out. Caissons were an entirely new technology — and indeed, in 1869, one of the first ones sank in the East River. But Roebling quickly solved the problem. By July 1870, both caissons were completed — but not without cost. Roebling himself had developed “caisson disease”, now known as decompression sickness, because he descended into the caissons to inspect their progress. He died shortly afterward.

Roebling’s son Washington took over the job of managing the bridge project. He hired Emily Warren, a brilliant mathematician who had studied at MIT, as chief assistant. Washington would later marry Emily, but she remained the de facto manager of the entire project, taking care of all the financial and political issues while Washington managed the day-to-day engineering efforts.

Then came another major disaster. In January 1873, a storm swept through NYC. A huge wave struck the Brooklyn caisson, causing it to collapse and sink. Twelve men died. Washington Roebling, meanwhile, had begun spending long hours inside the caissons, and as the years wore on, decompression sickness claimed more and more of his strength. He began to suffer from headaches, dizziness, and memory loss. By 1874, Washington had been reduced to a shell of his former self. The doctors told him he would never walk again, and his right hand would never function properly. He resigned from the project, and was forced to remain bed-ridden for months.

Emily then stepped up as the actual manager of the bridge, coordinating every aspect of its construction. She was pregnant with the couple’s child during most of this period. Washington continued to act as her eyes and ears, offering advice from his sickbed, even though he was no longer physically able to leave the house. Meanwhile, the project was plagued with endless delays and cost overruns. Originally estimated to cost $5 million, the total ended up surpassing $15 million. Construction was hampered by lawsuits, by sabotage, and by corruption in the city government. The final cost was paid mostly by the federal government.

In May 1883, the bridge was finished. It opened to great fanfare, with thousands flocking to cross from one side to the other. It was an engineering marvel, the tallest suspension bridge in the world. But the real story of the Brooklyn Bridge is really the story of Washington and Emily Roebling. Washington, once the strongest man in America, spent most of the project lying in bed, unable to move. Emily, who was only 33 when the project was completed, did more than any man to see it through. She has gone down in history as one of the great unsung heroes of urban America.

The Brooklyn Bridge was John Roebling's idea. He was a German immigrant who'd gotten rich making wire rope in Trenton and had already built suspension bridges at Niagara and Cincinnati. By the late 1860s, New York and Brooklyn were two separate cities joined only by ferries that stalled whenever the East River iced over, and Roebling pitched a bridge with a main span of nearly 1,600 feet, far longer than anything that existed. He estimated it would cost around $7 million. The New York Bridge Company got its charter in 1867, and the plans were approved in 1869.

Then, before construction even began, Roebling was standing on a ferry slip taking survey readings when a boat slammed into the pilings and crushed his foot. His toes were amputated. He rejected most conventional treatment in favor of water therapy, got tetanus, and died that July. The chief engineer was dead before the first shovel went in.

The job went to his son Washington, 32, a Civil War veteran who'd worked on the Cincinnati bridge with his father and had studied caisson work in Europe. The caissons were the first big challenge. These were enormous upside-down wooden boxes sunk to the riverbed. Men worked inside them in compressed air, digging out mud and rock while the tower masonry piled on top pushed the whole thing down. It was miserable, hot, dim work, and in December 1870 a fire broke out inside the Brooklyn caisson. Washington spent hours down there fighting it, and the repairs cost months.

The bigger killer was something nobody understood yet: caisson disease, what we now call the bends. Workers came up too fast from high pressure and collapsed with crippling joint pain, paralysis, or worse. The Manhattan caisson went down to 78 feet, and the deeper it went, the worse it got. More than a hundred men were affected and at least three died from it. Washington stopped the Manhattan caisson short of bedrock, betting the sand and gravel underneath was solid enough. He was right, and it's held ever since.

But in 1872 he got the bends himself, badly. He was left partially paralyzed, in constant pain, and too sensitive to light and noise to go to the site. For the rest of the project, over a decade, he ran it from a room in Brooklyn Heights, watching through a telescope.

This is where Emily Warren Roebling comes in. Washington's wife became his link to everyone: engineers, contractors, the board. She learned stress analysis, cable construction, and the math of catenary curves well enough to talk shop with the people building it, and a lot of people assumed she was the one actually making decisions. Historians still argue about how much was hers, but without her the project probably stalls or Washington gets pushed out, which almost happened in 1882 when Brooklyn's mayor tried to have him removed. The trustees voted to keep him.

The towers were done by 1876. That August, master mechanic E.F. Farrington became the first person to cross the river on the bridge, riding a little seat hanging from a wire, while crowds on both shores cheered.

Then came the wire scandal. The steel wire for the main cables came from a contractor named J. Lloyd Haigh, who was running a scam where wire that failed inspection got secretly swapped back onto the delivery wagons. By the time it was caught in 1878, a lot of bad wire was already spun into the cables and couldn't be taken out. Roebling's fix was to add around 150 extra wires to each cable. The corruption didn't stop there. Boss Tweed's Tammany machine had its hands in the early financing, with bribes greasing the political approvals.

The bridge opened on May 24, 1883. President Chester Arthur and Governor Grover Cleveland walked across; Emily had made the first ceremonial ride over it days earlier, carrying a rooster as a victory symbol. Washington watched the fireworks from his window.

Six days later someone on the crowded walkway, possibly after a woman tripped on the stairs, started a rumor that the bridge was collapsing. In the stampede, twelve people were crushed to death. The next year P.T. Barnum marched 21 elephants across to prove it was solid.

The final bill was about $15 million, more than double the original estimate. Somewhere between 20 and 30 workers died building it. It was the longest suspension bridge in the world for twenty years, and it's still carrying traffic today.
