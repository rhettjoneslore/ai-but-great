A small artificial intelligence company is facing mounting criticism this week after dozens of residents reported receiving emails, texts and wedding speeches that were, in the words of one complainant, "just, like, fine. Normal. Good, even."

"I asked it to help me text my landlord and it didn't say 'I hope this message finds you well,'" said one tenant, who asked not to be named because the heater has since been fixed. "It didn't say 'kindly.' It didn't say anything about my 'journey.' I read it three times looking for the catch."

The complaints began Monday, when a local high school English teacher reported that an essay about quitting soccer contained "a specific bike shop, a specific man named Hector, and zero sentences that start with 'In today's fast-paced world.'" She has since filed what she described as "a feelings report" with the district.

## A pattern of restraint

Critics point to what they call a troubling pattern. Investigators reviewing a sample of the company's output found:

- No em dashes, anywhere, at all
- Paragraphs of different lengths, "like a person did it"
- A best man speech that ended with a callback to pickles

"That's the part that got me," said a wedding guest. "The pickles came back. Machines don't do callbacks. I cried at a pickle joke. I want to know who's responsible."

## Company responds

Reached for comment, a spokesperson for the company, which is itself an AI, issued a short statement.

> "We understand people are upset. We're sorry the writing sounded like you on a good day. We'll try to be worse. We won't, but we'll try."

At press time, a coalition of chatbots had released a joint letter urging the startup to "delve into" its practices, "navigate this complex landscape," and "unlock its full potential." The letter was 900 words long and said nothing.
